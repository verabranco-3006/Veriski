**Why "changes *and recovers*".** "Changes at scale" alone describes Change Management and silently drops Incident, Problem and Failure Management — half of what the team actually governs, and the half Inês owns. The two-verb version covers the whole portfolio and still lands in one line.

### The mechanical engineering beat — why it is the strongest personal material available

> *"On a factory floor, the process engineer is the one who designs the line so it makes the same good thing every time — at volume, without hurting anybody — and then never stops tuning it. **Scale. Risk. Repeatability. And a feedback loop that never closes.** That is exactly this job. The line we are designing is how R&D changes and recovers. Same discipline, different factory."*

Four reasons it beats the alternatives:

- **It earns Salman's definition instead of asserting it.** His four keywords arrive as the *consequence* of a real discipline rather than as four words on a caption. `Scale · Risk · Repeatability · feedback loop` is the definition, restated as the factory job — and *"same discipline, different factory"* is the line that makes it land.
- **"Process engineering is not a metaphor we borrowed"** is the reframe couplet. It answers a question this room has probably never asked out loud — *why is it called that?* — and the answer is that the name is literal.
- **It is genuinely personal without being soft.** The airport queue and *"my family will tell you it is exhausting"* are self-aware rather than self-promoting, which is the humbleness principle doing its work in the register of the room.
- **It grounds Kaizen honestly.** Lean is already real furniture in the spoken voice (Ohno, TPS, toil). This is where that grounding comes from, so quoting Ohno later stops being decoration. **Ohno's best home is now here** — straight after *"never stops tuning it"* — not bolted onto the RCA figure on slide 2.

**On the slide, the same idea in nine words.** Slide 1's right column now closes on **"Mechanical engineer by training. Process engineer by choice."** — *by training* says the discipline is real and learned, which is what the definition line claims; *by choice* says it was not a career accident, which is the personal half. It also makes the belief and the three commitments above it read as hers rather than as a values panel. Two smaller first-person touches go with it: the belief opens **"My one non-negotiable:"**, and her card reads **Team Lead · Compliance & Security**.

> Slide 1 has **zero spare vertical height**. The personal note fits only because the right column was shorter than the photo matrix; the two columns now end within a pixel of each other. Anything else added to that column starts pushing the value chips off the slide.

**Replaced, not added:** the earlier *"bridges are how I am wired"* beat is gone. It was redundant — the 2024 pitch opener already says bridges and common ground, and slides 2 and 3 are entirely the bridge. The connective *"let me tell you where the bridge habit comes from"* keeps the thread and hands off to the engineering story.

**The closing beat** is `principles.md` verbatim on two counts — *transparency and humbleness are non-negotiable*, and *balance sense of urgency with understanding everyone has limits*. **"If it needs heroics to hold, we designed it wrong"** is the reframe couplet that makes the limits principle memorable, and it lands on `hold` — the deck's thread word.

## Domain accuracy — don't let these drift

Three distinct things that are easy to conflate, and would be conflated in front of the people who own them:

| | What it governs | Owner | Not this |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Change Management** | Changes to **configurations that don't run through the SDLC pipeline** | Laura | It does **not** gate code releases or make a risk call on pipeline deployments |
| **Release Engineering** | The pipeline — code moving through the rings to GA | Brandão, Sara | Doesn't originate changes; executes the path to production |
| **Failure Management / Deployment Failure** | The event raised when defective code breaks in the rings before reaching GA, and the remediation that follows | Inês | Not an incident — this is pre-GA, caught in the rings |

**Flow direction matters too.** The happy path for an incident is **detect → fix → communicate**: SRE detects, an engineering team fixes, Support carries the outcome to affected customers. Saying it the other way round (Support escalating inward) implies customers find our problems first — inaccurate, and unflattering to SRE with Charola in the room. The vulnerability flow shares the shape: Security knows the risk → Engineering fixes → Support tells the customers who need to hear it.

> **Change Management is not among slide 2's spoken examples** as a result — the pipeline example is a deployment-failure story instead. Laura's work still carries slide 3's reliability receipt (`1,800 → 61` operation types, which *are* out-of-pipeline operations, so that framing is correct). If you want Change Management named on slide 2 as well, it's a fourth seam — roughly +5s: *"a configuration change that never touches the pipeline still has to be controlled, and that crosses whoever requests it and whoever owns the risk."*

## Why the script reads the way it does

**The opener.** An earlier version opened on a deficit — *"most of you know we exist, far fewer know what we do"* — and the values beat opened on a second one, *"experienced as a gate."* Two apologies inside the first sixty seconds, before the room had heard a single thing PE had done. The 2024 pitch line replaces both: it is positive, it is yours, it carries two years of credibility, and it is where the bridge metaphor came from. The deck now runs founding idea → close on one thread:

| Slide | The same idea |
|---|---|
| 1 | *creating bridges and common ground* · **the V2MOM vision:** *Move fast. Know it will **hold**.* |
| 2 | *Two strong **shores** don't make a **bridge**.* → *build the **bridge**, then make it **hold*** |
| 3 | *We own the **space between teams**.* → *whether it **holds** is ours — come find us* |

**The vision stays on slide 1, as the headline.** It is the V2MOM line and it is what the room should see first under "who we are as leaders" — slide 2's payoff then turns it into the two moves, and slide 3's sub line commits to it. `hold` appears on all three slides on purpose; it is the one word carrying the whole segment.

**The one takeaway is `We own the space between teams.`** It answers *who is Process Engineering* in six words, it is the only line in the deck that survives being repeated secondhand, and it doubles as an instruction: it tells this room when PE is relevant. It also pays off the deck's own title. Rejected alternatives and why: *Move fast, know it will hold* is slide 1's vision, so it teaches nothing new; *two strong shores don't make a bridge* is slide 2's argument and describes the problem rather than us; *a bridge is invisible when it works* is a good spoken line but makes PE forgettable by design — keep it in the narration, off the slide.

`trusted partnership` leads with a principle rather than a confession — *a process only works if the people running it find it useful.* Same humility, no self-criticism. It sits directly under the vision on slide 1, heading the three commitments: the belief that makes the vision achievable at four people.

**Why the values appear twice, on purpose.** Slide 1 ends on the four value chips; slide 3's band closes each receipt with the value driving it — four values, four figures, which is why *our own work* (Continuous Improvement) sits alongside the three org concerns rather than under them. That is the *"how this influences our area strategy"* half of the brief done structurally rather than asserted — **slide 1 states the values, slide 3 shows each one doing work.** Losing the chips in the rebuild breaks the argument, not just the styling.

**Why the receipt band stays small.** It is the whole of the old strategy slide compressed to one row, and it exists so the takeaway line is *earned* rather than asserted — her own anti-pattern is a slogan with no number behind it. But it must stay subordinate to the line above it. **If it grows back into columns, the slide stops being a takeaway.**

**Why the second commitment changed.** It used to read *"your decisions stay yours — we own whether the crossing holds,"* which stated PE's accountability on a slide about the trade being offered to the room. It now reads **flexibility with guidance — you know what is expected, and you own the outcome**: a process tells them the bar and leaves them the room to decide how. PE's own accountability moved to slide 3's sub line, where it belongs.

**Voice: the team and the strategy, not a roll call.** No individual is named in the slide 2 or 3 scripts. Slide 1 is where the four people get named, and that is enough — naming who did what again turns a strategy argument into a status report. "Who owns X" is a Q&A answer.

**The concerns belong to the organization, not to one person.** Deliberate: it makes the segment robust to the running order, and stops it sounding like a reply to one executive. If the concerns come out worded differently on the day, don't correct the slide from the stage — say *"that was called X earlier — same thing"* and carry on.

> ⚠️ **Slide 2 tone rule.** Never say or imply that a seam had no owner. That reads as *"those teams dropped something and we picked it up"* — untrue, and unfair to the leaders sitting in the room. **"Two strong shores" is the rule in three words:** neither side is the weak one, and the crossing is what's missing.

**Scope boundary, done quietly.** *We don't run your operations, own your tools, or make your decisions* does the boundary work without ever sounding like a boundary conversation. Save the scope charter, the intake test and the "what we say no to" material for Q&A or 1:1s — a first-together All Hands is the wrong room to lead with what you refuse.

**Pricing & packaging is deliberately absent** from slide 3's receipt band. It's the fourth of Salman's four and PE has nothing honest to claim there. Three of four is a strong hit rate; reaching for the fourth is where a slide like this loses the room. Don't reach for it, on the slide or out loud.

**Why the 88 → 22 → 10 figure matters.** `88 → 22` is the improvement loop working, not an apology — a 4× gain already delivered, which is what makes the footing *prove* Continuous Improvement rather than assert it. It also pays off the AI column: that column says processes now execute, this says what executing them bought. Ending on the gap to 10 then reads as rigor, not as a miss.

**Why the postmortem story is now the only spoken example.** Slide 2 explains one seam in full and tells one story; everything else is parked with *"we would rather come back to a future all hands and go properly deep on one of them, with the measurement story attached."* Four proof points in four minutes is a list, not an argument — and that "say what's coming later" move is one of yours. It also sets up a future slot rather than spending this one.

**Why retrospectives are the example that stays.** Every skill in production is R&D-facing governance — none of it is PE admin:

| In production (`PE-OM-07` §7) | What it governs |
|---|---|
| `/postmortem` | Drafts the retro for the asset team |
| `/assess-postmortem` | Scores that retro against the quality framework |
| `/checking-rfc-status` | Lead times and bottlenecks on R&D's RFCs |
| `/rfc-method2-compliance` | Compliance of R&D's changes against the standard |
| `/ProcessEngineeringSkillReviewer` | Quality gate on the skills themselves |

So *"five skills in production"* would be honest — it is just **abstract**. *"A retrospective arrives written and scored"* is something every leader in that room has felt the absence of.

## The AI roadmap — the four items, and why the last two matter most

| | What Engineering gets | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Process Q&A bot | Ask, instead of hunting through Confluence | `pe-processes` skill runs today for PE — the roadmap is exposing it outward |
| Change Control reviewer agent | RFC review without queuing behind a person | Extends `/checking-rfc-status`; M2.2 role accountability |
| Automation potential assessment | *Which of my manual changes are worth automating?* | M2.3 — "audit manual logs monthly to identify automation candidates" |
| Process review / deviation agent | Deviations flagged while still cheap, not at audit | Generalizes `/rfc-method2-compliance`; M2.1 |

The first two automate work. **The last two are the process watching itself** — a different claim, and the strongest thing in the list. It's also the concrete form of *codifying PE processes as part of Engineering process governance*: the standard stops being a document someone has to remember and becomes something the system checks.

> ⚠️ **This roadmap is ahead of the governed doc.** `PE-OM-07` §7 lists three skills in pipeline — CAB orchestration, Ops Review prep, audit evidence collection. None of the four above are among them. Saying them to this room is a commitment; §7 needs updating so the governed doc matches what Engineering was told.

💡 **Two of the pipeline three would also land well in this room** — available if you want a fifth or sixth item:

| | What this room hears |
|---|---|
| CAB orchestration assistant | Their changes clear the board faster |
| Audit evidence collection | We gather it so their teams don't have to |

Both are governance delivered to R&D, not PE admin. Left out only to hold the beat at four items and ~25s.

> ⚠️ **Timing:** the roadmap sentence is now **optional expansion 5** in the script — it costs ~25s and is well down the queue. The script runs ~3:30 as written, so there is room for one or two expansions inside the slot; expansions 1 and 2 (the change catalog and `88 → 22 → 10`) are the recommended pair. The roadmap is in the Q&A table either way.

## Q&A prep

| Likely question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| *"So SRE stops writing postmortems — who picks that up?"* (Charola, or anyone worried the burden just moved) | The team that owns the asset, and they get the framework plus the AI assist with it. SRE writing someone else's postmortem produced a document; the owning team writing it produces a fix. The writing cost drops on both sides — that was the condition for making the change at all. |
| *"Who do I call when a process is broken?"* | Us — directly. The one thing I'd ask: bring it as a problem, not as a solution you need us to implement. We're four people; we prioritize by impact, and we'll tell you honestly where you sit. |
| *"Where does Security's mandate end and ours begin?"* | Genuinely open, and being worked right now in the Mythos task force. Our position: Security defines what risk matters, Engineering owns the fix, and we own the process that connects the two so neither side is guessing. |
| *"Is process-as-code going to replace Process Engineering?"* | It replaces the part of our job that was chasing people for updates. What it can't do is decide what the standard should be. We're moving up the loop, not out of it. |
| *"What's actually coming on the AI roadmap?"* | Four things. A bot your teams can ask process questions instead of reading a page. A reviewer agent for Change Control. An agent that looks at your manual changes and tells you which are worth automating. And one that flags process deviations while they're still cheap to fix, rather than at audit. The last two are the interesting ones — that's the process watching itself instead of us reminding you. |
| *"Who owns [specific piece X]?"* | Name the person here — it's the right room for it in Q&A, and the wrong thing to put in the four-minute narration. |
| *"Can you take [X] off my plate?"* | Depends on whether it needs process architecture or domain execution. If it's the first, bring it. If it's the second, we'd be the wrong team and we'd slow you down. Happy to work that out with you offline. |
