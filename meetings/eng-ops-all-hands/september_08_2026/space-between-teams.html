This frame is a proposal — it is ahead of the deck

Slides 2 and 3 mirror the export exactly. This one changes three things, all of them cheap to build — a text box, two lines, and one replaced sentence.

1 · Replace "At that ratio, trust is the operating model." That line no longer works: it used to follow "Four people — ODC and O11, for all of R&D", which supplied the ratio. The subtitle now gives the surface but never says four people, and the photos moved to slide 3 — so there is no ratio on the slide to refer back to. Replace it with "Three things you can hold me to." That fixes the dangling reference, signposts the count, and turns the three from a values list into personal promises.

2 · Tie the three to you, with two lines. One horizontal line from the right edge of your portrait, then one vertical line down the left side of the three commitments. That is the whole device: these three hang off me. It is also the same idiom as the bridge on slides 2 and 3 — a support with things hanging from it — so slide 1 now speaks the deck's visual language instead of being a text slide with a photo. Keep it thin and at partial opacity; it is structure, not a highlight.

3 · Under your name, two small lines: Team Lead, then under a thin rule, "Mechanical engineer by training. Process engineer by choice." Nine words carrying the origin story your narration spends 35 seconds on. Small and quiet — a margin note, not a headline.

The three are now stated in engineering language, not management language. Same three commitments, same source in principles.md — restated so they read as the instincts of the engineer the origin line describes:

1 · Diagnostics, not verdicts — metrics and compliance as diagnostic tools, not gatekeepers. Instrumentation is what an engineer calls a gauge, and this room is the one whose numbers get reported upward, so "not who to blame" is the half that matters to them.
2 · Tolerances, not instructions — autonomy with accountability. A spec gives you tolerances and leaves the method open, which is exactly the trade being offered. Keeps your phrase flexibility with guidance intact.
3 · If it doesn't carry load, it comes out — outcomes over activities. A structural-engineering sentence, falsifiable, and load and hold tie straight into the bridge on slides 2 and 3.
4 · Zero surprises, not zero problems — transparency and humbleness are non-negotiable. Zero surprises alone would be a boast; the turn is what makes it land. It concedes the failures in the same breath as it promises the disclosure, so transparency and humbleness are both delivered in five words without either word appearing. It is also the only line on the slide that admits something, which is what keeps the other three reading as confident rather than as marketing. And it covers more ground than a promise about audits: a process change will never land on their team unannounced, which is this room's actual friction with PE.

All four are now a couplet or a rule — no management phrasing, and the forms are mixed rather than uniform: couplet (3 words), couplet (3), rule (7), couplet (5). Four identical constructions would have read as a tic. The rule alternative for #4 is "If it surprises you, we got it wrong" — same content, pairs structurally with #3, slightly less memorable. The couplet wins because not zero problems is the part people repeat.

If you want only one couplet in the set, change #1 — not #2. Tolerances, not instructions is the best expression of "flexibility with guidance" available. A non-couplet #1 would be "We measure to find the slack", which echoes the origin paragraph directly, but in an ops room "finding the slack" can be heard as hunting for waste to cut, i.e. headcount. Diagnostics, not verdicts does the reassurance without that risk.

#4 was a spoken closing beat and is now a bullet. That is an upgrade: it was the strongest thing on slide 1 and it was invisible. The narration now runs "Fourth — no surprises. And this is the one I hold hardest…", so slide 1 ends on it rather than trailing off. The urgency-and-limits line ("if it needs heroics to hold, we designed it wrong") follows as a coda to all four rather than sitting inside #4 — it is a different principle and it dilutes the transparency point if merged.

Why this beats the management phrasing it replaced. Each head is now a reframe couplet — X, not Y — which is the move your spoken voice already makes ("blameless does not mean nameless"). More importantly the triad stops being a values list any leader could show and becomes the behavioural signature of the person in the photo beside it. The origin line, the tie and the three heads now all say the same thing, which is what makes the slide personal without a single extra sentence.

Audience check: this is an engineering-ops room — SRE, Release Engineering, Quality Engineering. Tolerances and carries load are their vocabulary, not jargon you are importing. In a non-engineering room the plain-language heads would be the safer choice.

The surroundings do the personal work too. "Hold me to" makes them promises with a name attached, the tie makes them visibly yours rather than a list that could belong to any team, and the origin line says where they came from.

"me" or "us" — your call, and it is a real one. writing_style.md reserves "I" for facilitation and uses "we" for team work, so "hold me to" is a deliberate exception: on a slide titled who we are as leaders, a leader owning the promise is the point, and it is the most personal word available at no cost. "Hold us to" is the safer variant, matches the rest of the script's voice, and still works with the tie and the origin line. The accent sits on that one word either way, so swapping it is a one-word edit.

The V2MOM values are deliberately not here. An earlier draft paired each commitment with one of Operational Excellence / Automation First / Trusted Partnership and stood them on a Continuous Improvement footing. It was accurate but it made the slide a mapping exercise, and the three commitments are the values this room needs. The four remain in the narration and in the V2MOM; they are not on any slide.

Mirrored from the export, unchanged: the vision headline with Know it will hold in the accent, the definition subtitle, your portrait, and the three numbered commitments in blue circles. Commitment 2's rename to "Accountable autonomy" is carried through to the script.

Still worth adding "four people" to the subtitle — "…outcomes predictable. Four people, ODC and O11, all of R&D." The scale mismatch is the strongest single fact you have, the photos no longer supply it, and it costs four words. Independent of the changes above.

One typo to fix in the deck

"What stops from happening again?" is missing a word — it should read "What stops it from happening again?" Corrected in this reference. It is the third question in the blue box.

The single-pylon bridge is a better drawing than the two-tower version it replaced. One pylon carrying one continuous deck across shores that never touch is exactly the argument, and it sets up slide 3, where Process Engineering is labelled at the foot of that same pylon. Keep the pylon crossing below the deck line — that overhang is what slide 3 hangs the team off.

The questions in one box in the middle is the whole idea. Both accountabilities stay visible on either side, the question sits physically in the gap between them, and the connector line runs from each team to the box edge. Team names in the accent, what-they-own in the strong text colour, questions in the blue.

Tone rule, because the layout enforces it: every row shows both accountabilities on screen, on purpose. Nothing here may imply a seam had no owner — that would read as "those teams dropped something and we picked it up," which is untrue and unfair to the leaders in the room. Two strong shores is the whole rule in three words: neither side is the weak one.

"Build the bridge, make it hold" replaced the payoff paragraph, and it is the better trade — it is the two moves in six words, and hold ties back to slide 1's vision. The reasoning that paragraph carried ("bridging that gap is the work, and it is ours") is spoken instead.

Change Management is deliberately not one of the three rows. It governs configuration changes outside the SDLC pipeline — it does not gate code releases. Laura's work is named in the spoken reliability example instead (1,800 → 61 operation types, which are out-of-pipeline operations, so that framing is accurate). If you want it named here it is a fourth row, roughly +5s.

Two things I'd change back

1 · "Come find us" is gone, and it was the payload. The takeaway used to end "Standing between two teams with a question neither side can answer alone? That is not friction you have to live with. Come find us." Without it the slide states an identity; with it, the slide gives the room an instruction they can act on — which is the whole reason this is the last slide. It is one line and there is white space for it between the sub and the bridge. It is still in the script, so at minimum say it out loud; better, put it back on the slide.

2 · "space between the teams" → "space between teams." Dropping the makes it a category rather than a specific set of teams, which is what you want in a line built to be repeated secondhand. It also scans better. And the headline is missing its full stop while the line above and below both have one.

Labelling the pylon "Process Engineering" is the best idea in the deck. The team is not on the shores and not on the deck — it is the structure in the middle holding the span up, and the four of you hang directly off it. That says we own the space between teams better than the sentence does. Do not let this get rebuilt as a logo or a footer; the label has to sit at the foot of the pylon, and the crew box has to sit under the label.

The photos work here in a way they would not on slide 1. On the opening slide they read as an org chart; here, arriving after the claim and hanging off the pylon, they read as these are the four people who do that. Moving them was right.

Keep it spare. Kicker, claim, one sub line, the bridge, the label, the faces. No figures — every number is spoken on slide 2, which means they can be softened or dropped live without a slide contradicting the speaker, and it keeps the takeaway from becoming a scoreboard. Resist adding receipts here.
